Haben und Sein / Regelmäßige Verben

The present tense: haben and sein, the regular verb pattern, and every spelling adjustment

Original topic sheet View "Verben Konjugation" in the Picture Book →

To konjugieren (conjugate) a verb means to give it the right ending for the person doing the action. In English this barely happens: I learn, you learn, we learn — only he learns is different. In German every person has its own ending, and a verb without the right ending sounds as wrong as English "he learn". The good news is that most German verbs follow one single pattern. Learn that pattern, plus the two special verbs haben and sein, and you can already say a huge amount.

1. The present tense does three jobs

German has one present tense, the Präsens, where English has several. Ich lerne can mean all of these:

One German form, several English meanings

GermanEnglishMeaning
Ich lerne jeden Tag.I learn every day.A habit
Ich lerne gerade.I am learning right now.Happening now (gerade = right now)
Ich lerne morgen.I'm going to learn tomorrow.The future, with a time word
Lernst du Deutsch?Do you learn / Are you learning German?A question: no "do" in German

Don't build "am learning" or "do learn"

German has no -ing form and no helper do. "Ich bin lernen" and "Ich tue lernen" are both wrong. It is simply Ich lerne.

2. sein (to be)

sein is completely irregular. There is no pattern: learn its six forms by heart, today, until you can say them without thinking.

sein — present tense

PronounseinEnglish
ichbinI am
dubistyou are
er / sie / esisthe / she / it is
wirsindwe are
ihrseidyou (all) are
sie / Siesindthey are / you are (formal)

What sein is used for

  • Name and identity: Ich bin Grace. (I'm Grace.)
  • Job (no article): Er ist Koch. (He is a cook.)
  • Origin: Wir sind aus Ruanda. (We are from Rwanda.)
  • Age: Sie ist 19 Jahre alt. (She is 19 years old.)
  • Description: Das Wetter ist schön. (The weather is nice.)
  • Location: Ihr seid zu Hause. (You are at home.)

seid or seit?

They sound identical. ihr seid (you all are) is the verb. seit (since, for) is a time word: Ich wohne seit zwei Jahren hier. (I've lived here for two years.) Mixing them up is one of the most common spelling mistakes, even among Germans.

3. haben (to have)

haben is only slightly irregular: the b disappears in the du and er/sie/es forms.

haben — present tense

PronounhabenEnglish
ichhabeI have
duhastyou have
er / sie / eshathe / she / it has
wirhabenwe have
ihrhabtyou (all) have
sie / Siehabenthey have / you have (formal)

What haben is used for

  • Possession and family: Ich habe ein Auto und zwei Kinder. (I have a car and two children.)
  • Time: Hast du heute Zeit? (Do you have time today?)
  • Feelings and needs, where English uses "be": Ich habe Hunger. (I'm hungry.) · Er hat Durst. (He's thirsty.) · Sie hat Angst. (She is afraid.)
  • Symptoms: Wir haben Kopfschmerzen. (We have headaches.)
  • Birthdays: Heute habe ich Geburtstag. (It's my birthday today.)

haben vs sein: the swaps

  • Age uses sein: Ich bin 30. Never "Ich habe 30 Jahre."
  • Hunger, thirst and fear use haben: Ich habe Hunger, not "Ich bin hungrig". The adjective exists, but the haben version is what people actually say.

5. Spelling adjustments

The pattern never changes, but a few stems need a small spelling adjustment so the word can still be pronounced. There are four groups.

Group A: stems ending in -t or -d → add an -e-

Try saying "arbeitst". It's almost impossible. So before the endings -st and -t, German adds an e:

arbeiten (to work)warten (to wait)finden (to find)
icharbeitewartefinde
duarbeitestwartestfindest
er/sie/esarbeitetwartetfindet
wirarbeitenwartenfinden
ihrarbeitetwartetfindet
sie/Siearbeitenwartenfinden

The same happens with stems ending in a consonant + m or n: öffnen (to open) → du öffnest, er öffnet; rechnen (to calculate) → er rechnet; atmen (to breathe) → sie atmet; and es regnet (it's raining).

Group B: stems ending in an s-sound → du just adds -t

If the stem already ends in -s, -ss, -ß, -z, -tz or -x, the du form drops the extra s. That makes it identical to the er form.

Verbduer/sie/es
heißen (to be called)du heißter heißt
tanzen (to dance)du tanztsie tanzt
reisen (to travel)du reister reist
sitzen (to sit)du sitztes sitzt

Group C: verbs ending in -eln and -ern

These infinitives end in -n, not -en. So wir and sie add only -n, and the ich form usually drops the e of the stem.

wandern (to hike)sammeln (to collect)
ichwand(e)resammle
duwanderstsammelst
er/sie/eswandertsammelt
wirwandernsammeln

Group D: verbs ending in -ieren are perfectly regular

Many international verbs end in -ieren, and they are all completely regular: studieren (to study at university) → ich studiere, du studierst, er studiert. Others: telefonieren (to phone), reparieren (to repair), funktionieren (to work, function), fotografieren (to photograph).

A correction to the topic sheet: helfen is not regular

The sheet lists helfen (to help) among verbs "with no change", but its own table shows du hilfst, er hilft: the stem vowel changes from e to i. The endings are still regular, but the vowel is not. Verbs like this are the topic of Chapter 8. If a verb's du form has a different vowel from the infinitive, it belongs there, not here.

7. A text full of verbs

Familie Mensah

Das ist Familie Mensah. Sie wohnt in Dortmund. Kofi arbeitet in einer Werkstatt, und seine Frau Abena studiert Medizin. Sie haben zwei Kinder. Die Kinder heißen Ama und Yaw. Ama ist acht und spielt gern Fußball. Yaw sammelt Briefmarken. Am Wochenende wandern sie oft, oder sie besuchen Freunde. Und du? Was machst du am Wochenende?

(This is the Mensah family. They live in Dortmund. Kofi works in a workshop, and his wife Abena studies medicine. They have two children. The children are called Ama and Yaw. Ama is eight and likes playing football. Yaw collects stamps. At the weekend they often go hiking, or they visit friends. And you? What do you do at the weekend?)
