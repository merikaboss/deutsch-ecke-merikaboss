Bestimmte Artikel im Nominativ

Definite articles: der, die, das — and grammatical gender

Original topic sheet View "Bestimmter Artikel" in the Picture Book →

This is the start of the case system — arguably the biggest structural difference between German and English. It gets built up gradually: this chapter covers only the nominative (subject) case. Chapters 19, 26, and 32 add the accusative and dative cases on top of what you learn here.

Grammar rule: every German noun has a gender

Every noun is grammatically masculine (der), feminine (die), or neuter (das) — regardless of whether the thing itself has a real-world gender. A table (der Tisch) is grammatically "masculine"; a door (die Tür) is grammatically "feminine." This has nothing to do with biology — it's just a category every noun belongs to, and it's fixed. All plural nouns, regardless of original gender, use die.

GenderArticleExample
Masculinederder Mann (the man), der Tisch (the table)
Femininediedie Frau (the woman), die Tür (the door)
Neuterdasdas Kind (the child), das Auto (the car)
Plural (any gender)diedie Männer, die Frauen, die Autos (the men, women, cars)

Common mistake: guessing gender from meaning

das Mädchen (the girl) is grammatically neuter, not feminine — because any noun ending in -chen is automatically neuter, no matter what it refers to. Gender is a grammatical category attached to the word, not a reflection of real-world sex. There's no way to reliably guess it from meaning alone.

A few patterns that DO help predict gender

There's no perfect rule, but these patterns are reliable enough to be worth learning:

PatternGenderExample
Male people and animalsderder Vater, der Löwe
Female people and animalsdiedie Mutter, die Katze
Days, months, seasonsderder Montag, der Mai, der Winter
Ends in -e (often)diedie Lampe, die Tasche
Ends in -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -tiondiedie Zeitung, die Freiheit, die Nation
Ends in -chen, -leindasdas Mädchen, das Fräulein
Infinitive verb used as a noundasdas Lernen (learning, as a noun)

The single most important habit for German learners

Never learn a noun without its article. Don't write "Tisch" in your notes — write der Tisch. This one habit, repeated for months, is what eventually makes gender feel automatic instead of memorized.

Capitalization: a genuinely useful rule

Unlike English, every German noun is capitalized, not just proper names: der Tisch, not "der tisch." This is actually helpful once you're used to it — spotting capital letters mid-sentence tells you instantly which words are nouns.

Exercises

Try it yourself

1. Give the correct article (der/die/das) for: Buch, Frau, Auto, Mann, Zeitung.
2. Why is das Mädchen neuter even though it means "girl"? What's the rule involved?
3. What article do ALL plural nouns take, regardless of original gender?
4. Which of these endings usually signal a feminine noun: -ung, -chen, -heit, -lein?
5. Write out these nouns exactly as you should learn them, with articles: table, door, child, newspaper.
