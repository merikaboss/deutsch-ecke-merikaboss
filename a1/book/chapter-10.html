Bestimmte Artikel (alle Fälle)

der, die, das: gender, plural, and the definite article in Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ and Genitiv

Original topic sheet View "Bestimmter Artikel" in the Picture Book →

This is the most important grammar chapter in the book. English has one word for "the". German has six — der, die, das, den, dem, des — and which one you use depends on three things: the noun's gender, whether it is singular or plural, and its case, meaning its job in the sentence. That sounds like a lot, but it is one system with a clear logic, and everything later in the book (ein, kein, mein, pronouns, adjective endings) follows the same pattern. Take this chapter slowly. It pays you back in every chapter after it.

1. What the article tells you

The bestimmter Artikel (definite article) means "the": a specific thing the listener can identify. In German it does more than English "the". It carries information:

One article, three pieces of information

In Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch. (I give the man the book.), the word dem tells you: the noun is masculine, singular, and in the dative case — the person receiving something. A German speaker hears all of that before the word Mann arrives.

2. Gender: der, die, das

Every noun has a grammatical gender

GenderArticleExamples
maskulin (masculine)derder Mann (man), der Tisch (table), der Hund (dog)
feminin (feminine)diedie Frau (woman), die Tür (door), die Katze (cat)
neutral (neuter)dasdas Kind (child), das Auto (car), das Buch (book)

Gender belongs to the word, not to the thing. A table isn't male and a door isn't female; they are just in those word groups. That's why you must learn every noun together with its article, as one unit: never "Tisch", always der Tisch.

Common mistake: guessing gender from meaning

das Mädchen (the girl) is neuter, because every noun ending in -chen is neuter, whatever it means. die Person is feminine even for a man, and der Mensch (the human being) is masculine even for a woman. The word's form decides, not the real world.

Patterns that predict gender

There's no rule for every noun, but these patterns are reliable and cover a large share of the vocabulary. They are worth learning, especially the endings.

Usually der

  • Male people and animals: der Vater, der Onkel, der Löwe (father, uncle, lion)
  • Days, months, seasons: der Montag, der Mai, der Winter
  • Weather and directions: der Regen, der Schnee, der Norden (rain, snow, north)
  • People who do something, ending in -er: der Lehrer, der Fahrer (teacher, driver)
  • Endings -ling, -ismus, -ist: der Frühling, der Tourismus, der Polizist (spring, tourism, police officer)
  • Most alcoholic drinks: der Wein, der Whisky — but das Bier

Usually die

  • Female people and animals: die Mutter, die Tante, die Kuh (mother, aunt, cow)
  • Female job forms with -in: die Lehrerin, die Ärztin
  • Always with -ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -tion, -ität: die Zeitung, die Freiheit, die Möglichkeit, die Freundschaft, die Information, die Universität (newspaper, freedom, possibility, friendship, information, university)
  • Endings -ie, -ik, -ei, -ur: die Familie, die Musik, die Bäckerei, die Natur
  • Most two-syllable nouns ending in -e: die Lampe, die Tasche, die Straße (lamp, bag, street)
  • Numbers as nouns: die Eins, die Zehn

Usually das

  • Always with -chen and -lein: das Mädchen, das Brötchen, das Fräulein
  • Endings -ment, -um, -o: das Dokument, das Museum, das Auto, das Radio
  • Verbs used as nouns: das Essen (eating, food), das Schwimmen (swimming)
  • Young people and animals: das Kind, das Baby, das Kalb (child, baby, calf)
  • Most countries and cities: das schöne Deutschland, das alte Berlin (normally used without the article)
  • Metals and colours as nouns: das Gold, das Rot

Compound nouns: the last part decides

German builds long nouns by joining shorter ones. The last word always gives the gender of the whole:

  • das Haus + die Tür = die Haustür (front door)
  • die Hand + der Schuh = der Handschuh (glove)
  • der Kaffee + die Tasse = die Kaffeetasse (coffee cup)

These patterns are the short version. Chapter 56 — Der, die oder das? goes through every reliable ending, the exceptions that actually come up at A1, tricks for when you are not sure, and a word list of over 600 everyday nouns sorted by gender.

3. Plural: always die

Here is some good news: in the plural, the gender disappears. Every plural noun, whatever its original gender, takes die in the nominative. The noun itself, however, changes form in one of five ways, and you learn each noun's plural along with it.

The five plural patterns

PatternSingularPlural
-e (sometimes with umlaut)der Tisch · der Stuhldie Tische · die Stühle
-er (umlaut where possible)das Kind · das Buchdie Kinder · die Bücher
-n / -endie Lampe · die Fraudie Lampen · die Frauen
-s (mostly foreign words)das Auto · das Handydie Autos · die Handys
no ending (sometimes umlaut)der Lehrer · die Mutterdie Lehrer · die Mütter

Dictionaries show this in shorthand: der Tisch, -e means the plural is Tische; das Buch, ¨-er means Bücher. Two helpful tendencies: nouns ending in -ung, -heit, -keit and most feminine nouns take -en, and nouns ending in -er, -el, -chen usually add nothing.

Plural die is not feminine

Seeing die Bücher, learners sometimes think Buch is feminine. It isn't: it's das Buch, and die appears only because it's plural. Always learn the singular with its article first.

4. The four cases: what a "case" is

A Fall or Kasus (case) is the role a noun plays in a sentence. English still has cases, but only in pronouns: he sees; I see him; that is his book. Or who / whom / whose. German does the same thing with every article.

Die vier Fälle (the four cases)

CaseRoleTest questionEnglish trace
Nominativthe subject: who or what does the actionWer? Was? (who? what?)he, who
Akkusativthe direct object: who or what receives the actionWen? Was? (whom? what?)him, whom
Dativthe indirect object: to or for whomWem? (to whom?)(to) him
Genitivpossession: whoseWessen? (whose?)his, whose

Finding the case with the test questions

Der Lehrer gibt dem Schüler den Stift des Kindes. (The teacher gives the pupil the child's pen.)

QuestionAnswerCase
Wer gibt? (Who gives?)der LehrerNominativ
Was gibt er? (What does he give?)den StiftAkkusativ
Wem gibt er den Stift? (To whom?)dem SchülerDativ
Wessen Stift? (Whose pen?)des KindesGenitiv

The memory chant

Say the table row by row, out loud, until it's automatic, as German schoolchildren do:

  • Nominativ: der – die – das – die
  • Akkusativ: den – die – das – die
  • Dativ: dem – der – dem – den
  • Genitiv: des – der – des – der

Three shortcuts make it much lighter: (1) in the accusative only the masculine changes; (2) masculine and neuter are identical in the dative and genitive; (3) feminine is die twice, then der twice.

6. Nominativ: the subject

The nominative is the dictionary form. It's used for the subject, and also for the noun after sein (to be), werden (to become) and heißen (to be called), because both sides name the same thing:

Nominativ examples

  • Der Mann ist müde. (The man is tired.)
  • Die Frau liest ein Buch. (The woman is reading a book.)
  • Das Kind spielt. (The child is playing.)
  • Die Kinder singen. (The children are singing.)
  • Das ist der Chef. (That is the boss.) — after sein

7. Akkusativ: the direct object

Only der changes: der → den

NominativAkkusativExample
der Hundden HundIch sehe den Hund. (I see the dog.)
die Katzedie KatzeIch sehe die Katze.
das Pferddas PferdIch sehe das Pferd.
die Vögeldie VögelIch sehe die Vögel. (birds)

Most verbs that take an object take the accusative. Common ones: haben (have), sehen (see), kaufen (buy), brauchen (need), suchen (look for), nehmen (take), essen (eat), trinken (drink), finden (find), besuchen (visit), lieben (love), fragen (ask). Also es gibt (there is): Es gibt den Park hier nicht mehr. (The park isn't here any more.)

Prepositions that always take the Akkusativ

PrepositionMeaningExample

Memory word: DOGFU — durch, ohne, gegen, für, um.

8. Dativ: the indirect object

dem – der – dem – den + plural -n

NominativDativExample
der Vaterdem VaterIch helfe dem Vater. (I help the father.)
die Mutterder MutterIch danke der Mutter. (I thank the mother.)
das Kinddem KindDas Buch gehört dem Kind. (The book belongs to the child.)
die Kinderden KindernIch gebe den Kindern Schokolade. (I give the children chocolate.)

Plural dative adds -n to the noun, unless the plural already ends in -n or -s: den Frauen, den Autos.

When the dative is used

(a) Verbs with a person receiving something — dative person, accusative thing:

  • Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch. (I give the man the book.)
  • Also: schenken (give as a present), zeigen (show), schicken (send), erklären (explain), bringen (bring)

(b) Verbs that take only the dative — learn these, because English uses a direct object:

  • helfen (help), danken (thank), antworten (answer), gehören (belong to), gefallen (please), gratulieren (congratulate), schmecken (taste good to), passen (fit, suit)

(c) After prepositions that always take the dative:

PrepositionMeaningExample
ausout of, fromaus dem Haus
beiat, with (someone's place)bei der Oma (at grandma's)
mitwith, by (transport)mit dem Bus (by bus)
nachafter; to (cities, countries)nach dem Essen (after the meal)
seitsince, for (time)seit dem Sommer
vonfrom, ofvon der Arbeit
zuto (people, places)zu dem Arzt = zum Arzt
gegenüberoppositegegenüber dem Bahnhof

Common mistakes with the dative

  • "mit der Bus" → mit dem Bus. After mit always dative; der Bus becomes dem Bus.
  • "Ich helfe den Mann" → Ich helfe dem Mann. helfen takes the dative.
  • "mit den Kinder" → mit den Kindern. Don't forget the plural -n.

9. Genitiv: possession

des – der – des – der + -(e)s

NominativGenitivExample
der Vaterdes Vatersdas Auto des Vaters (the father's car)
die Mutterder Mutterdie Tasche der Mutter (the mother's bag)
das Kinddes Kindesdas Zimmer des Kindes (the child's room)
die Kinderder Kinderdie Spielsachen der Kinder (the children's toys)

The owner comes after the thing owned: "the car of the father". Masculine and neuter nouns add -s, or -es for most one-syllable words and words ending in -s, -ß, -z: des Mannes, des Hauses. Feminine and plural nouns add nothing.

Three ways to show "whose" — and which to use when

FormExampleWhere you'll meet it
Genitivedas Auto des NachbarnWriting, news, official texts, careful speech
von + dativedas Auto von dem Nachbarn = vom NachbarnVery common in everyday speech
Name + -sAnnas AutoWith names: no apostrophe, name before the noun

Question: Wessen Auto ist das? — Das ist das Auto des Nachbarn. (Whose car is that? — It's the neighbour's car.)

Prepositions with the Genitiv

  • wegen (because of): wegen des Wetters (because of the weather)
  • während (during): während der Arbeit
  • trotz (despite): trotz des Regens
  • statt / anstatt (instead of): statt des Autos

In relaxed speech many Germans use the dative after these (wegen dem Wetter), but written German uses the genitive. Learn the genitive; you'll understand both.

No apostrophe, and a special group of nouns

  • "Anna's Buch" is wrong in standard German: Annas Buch. (You'll see the apostrophe on shop signs, but it isn't correct.)
  • A small group of masculine nouns, mostly people and animals, adds -n / -en in every case except the nominative: der Student → den / dem / des Studenten, der Herr → Herrn, der Junge → Jungen, der Kollege → Kollegen, der Mensch → Menschen, der Nachbar → Nachbarn, der Kunde → Kunden. This is called the n-Deklination. You've seen it: Sehr geehrter Herr Braun, but Ich spreche mit Herrn Braun.

10. Prepositions that take two cases

Nine prepositions of place can take either the dative or the accusative. The rule is about location versus movement, and it's one of the most useful rules in German.

Wo? → Dativ  ·  Wohin? → Akkusativ

The nine: in (in, into), an (at, on — vertical), auf (on — horizontal), über (over, above), unter (under), vor (in front of), hinter (behind), neben (next to), zwischen (between).

Wo? Where is it? (Dativ)Wohin? Where is it going? (Akkusativ)
Ich bin im Kino. (I'm at the cinema.)Ich gehe ins Kino. (I'm going to the cinema.)
Das Buch liegt auf dem Tisch. (The book is lying on the table.)Ich lege das Buch auf den Tisch. (I put the book on the table.)
Das Bild hängt an der Wand. (The picture hangs on the wall.)Ich hänge das Bild an die Wand. (I hang the picture on the wall.)

Short forms: article + preposition

The short forms are normal in speech and writing: Ich gehe zum Arzt und dann zur Bank. (I'm going to the doctor and then to the bank.)

11. When to use the definite article

Use der / die / das when ...

  • the thing is specific or already known: Ich sehe den Hund. (I see the dog — the one we both know.)
  • it's mentioned for the second time: Ich habe ein Buch. Das Buch ist neu.
  • there's only one: die Sonne, der Mond (the sun, the moon)
  • with days, months and seasons after am / im: am Montag, im Mai, im Winter
  • with some countries: die Schweiz, die Türkei, die USA
  • often with general ideas where English has none: Das Leben ist schön. (Life is beautiful.)

No article with names (Anna kommt), jobs after sein (Er ist Lehrer), languages (Ich lerne Deutsch), and uncountable amounts (Ich trinke Wasser).

12. All four cases in one story

Der Geburtstag (the birthday)

Die MutterN kauft den KuchenA in der BäckereiD. Der VaterN schenkt dem KindD das FahrradA. Das Fahrrad ist ein Geschenk der GroßelternG. Die KinderN singen, und den KindernD schmeckt der KuchenN. Nach dem EssenD gehen alle in den ParkA.

(The mother buys the cake at the bakery. The father gives the child the bicycle. The bicycle is a present from the grandparents. The children sing, and the children enjoy the cake. After the meal everyone goes to the park.) N = Nominativ, A = Akkusativ, D = Dativ, G = Genitiv. Notice schmecken: the cake is the subject, and the children who enjoy it are dative.
