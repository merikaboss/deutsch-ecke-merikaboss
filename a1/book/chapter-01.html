Greetings, introducing yourself, and getting to know someone

Saying hello: it depends on the time of day

German has different greetings depending on when you're saying them — unlike English "hi," which works any time. Learn all four; you'll hear each one constantly.

GermanEnglishWhen
Guten Morgen!Good morning!Early, until roughly 10–11am
Guten Tag!Good day! / Hello!Daytime, the safe default
Guten Abend!Good evening!From early evening onward
Gute Nacht!Good night!Only when someone is going to bed — not a greeting, a farewell

In casual speech, especially among young people or friends, all of that gets skipped in favor of one word: Hallo! (hello!). There's also Servus (hi / bye — informal), common in southern Germany and Austria, and Grüß dich / grüß Gott (greetings to you — informal, regional).

📐 Formal vs. informal — why it matters here

German has two ways of saying "you": du (you — informal) for friends, family, children, and people your own age you've just met casually, and Sie (you — formal, always capitalized) for strangers, officials, older people, and any professional context. You'll learn the full grammar of this in Chapter 6 (Personalpronomen), but it already affects which greeting sounds natural: Hallo and Servus are strictly informal. Guten Tag works for everyone and is the safest choice with someone you don't know.

Asking "how are you?"

The literal question is Wie geht es dir? (how are you? — informal) or Wie geht es Ihnen? (how are you? — formal). In speech this almost always shortens to Wie geht's? (how's it going?).

GermanEnglish
Mir geht es gut, danke.I'm doing well, thanks.
Danke, gut. Und dir?Good, thanks. And you? (informal)
Es geht so.It's okay / so-so.
Nicht so gut.Not so good.
Sehr gut, danke!Very good, thanks!

⚠️ Common mistake: "Ich bin gut"

English speakers instinctively want to answer "how are you" with "I am good" and translate it word-for-word as Ich bin gut. Don't — that actually means "I am a good person" (a statement about your character, not your wellbeing). The correct pattern uses mir (to/for me — a dative pronoun, covered properly in Chapter 19): Mir geht es gut, literally "it goes well for me." For now, just memorize the phrase as a whole chunk — the grammar behind it will make sense later.

Introducing yourself

Three common ways to say who you are, from most to least common in everyday speech:

💬 Examples

  • Ich heiße Anna. (My name is Anna — literally "I am called Anna.")
  • Ich bin Anna. (I am Anna.)
  • Mein Name ist Anna. (My name is Anna — slightly more formal.)

To ask someone else's name:

GermanEnglish
Wie heißt du?What's your name? (informal)
Wie heißen Sie?What's your name? (formal)
Wie heißt ihr?What are your (plural) names? (informal, to a group)

Notice the verb heißen (to be called) changes its ending depending on who's speaking — ich heiße, du heißt, Sie heißen. This pattern (called conjugation) applies to every German verb and gets its own full explanation in Chapter 7 — for these greeting phrases, just learn them as set expressions for now.

Getting to know someone: where are you from, where do you live

QuestionEnglishAnswerEnglish
Woher kommst du? Where are you from? Ich komme aus Uganda. I come from Uganda.
Wo wohnst du? Where do you live? Ich wohne in Kampala. I live in Kampala.
Was machst du? What do you do? (job/right now) Ich lerne Deutsch. I'm learning German.

⚠️ Common mistake: aus vs in

Woher kommst du? pairs with aus (from): aus Uganda. But Wo wohnst du? pairs with in: in Kampala. Mixing these up ("Ich wohne aus...") is one of the first small errors English speakers make, because English uses "from" more loosely.

Saying goodbye

GermanEnglish
Auf Wiedersehen!Goodbye! (formal, or on the phone)
Tschüss!Bye! (informal, very common)
Bis bald!See you soon!
Bis später!See you later!
Gute Nacht!Good night! (when someone's going to sleep)

A short, complete dialogue

💬 Example — two people meeting for the first time

A: Hallo! Ich heiße Lisa. Und du?
A: Hi! My name is Lisa. And you?

B: Hallo Lisa! Ich bin Tom. Wie geht's?
B: Hi Lisa! I'm Tom. How's it going?

A: Gut, danke! Woher kommst du?
A: Good, thanks! Where are you from?

B: Ich komme aus Berlin. Und du, wo wohnst du?
B: I'm from Berlin. And you, where do you live?

A: Ich wohne in München. Schön, dich kennenzulernen!
A: I live in Munich. Nice to meet you!

B: Schön, dich auch kennenzulernen! Bis bald!
B: Nice to meet you too! See you soon!

Exercises

✏️ Try it yourself

1. It's 8pm and you're greeting a colleague at a work event. Which greeting fits best — Guten Morgen, Guten Tag, or Guten Abend?
2. Fill in the blank: ___ geht es dir? — ___ geht es gut, danke.
3. Write two different ways to say "My name is [your name]" in German.
4. Ask a stranger their name, using the formal form.
5. Translate into German: "Where do you come from? I come from Kenya. Where do you live?"
6. True or false: Tschüss is appropriate when saying goodbye to a judge in a courtroom.
7. Spot the mistake and correct it: "Wie geht es? Ich bin gut."
8. Write a short 4-line dialogue introducing yourself to a new classmate, including at least one greeting, your name, and where you're from.
