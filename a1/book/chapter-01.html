Begrüßungen, Vorstellen und Kennenlernen

Greetings, everyday phrases, introducing yourself and others, and getting to know someone

Original topic sheet View "Begrüßungen, Vorstellen und Kennenlernen" in the Picture Book →

Everything in German starts here. In your first real conversation you will greet someone, say your name, say where you come from and where you live, ask the same things back, and say goodbye. This chapter gives you all of that, plus the small polite words (please, thank you, sorry) that you will use every single day. It is also exactly what the first part of the A1 speaking exam tests.

1. Before anything else: du or Sie?

English has one word for "you". German has two, and choosing correctly matters more than grammar at first, because the wrong one can sound rude or oddly cold.

The two forms of "you"

FormCalledUse it with
du informal Friends, family, children, classmates, people your own age in casual settings, animals
Sie formal, always with a capital S Strangers, adults you don't know, shop and office staff, doctors, teachers, your boss, anyone older in a formal setting

When in doubt, use Sie. Nobody is offended by politeness, and if the other person prefers du they will say Wir können uns duzen. (We can say "du" to each other.)

Many phrases in this chapter come in two versions, one for each form. You will see that the verb changes too: Wie heißt du? but Wie heißen Sie? Both mean "What's your name?" The full reason is in Chapters 6 and 7; for now, learn each pair together.

2. Begrüßungen (greetings)

German greetings depend on the time of day, unlike English "hi", which works at any hour.

Greetings through the day

GermanEnglishWhen
Guten Morgen!Good morning!From waking up until about 10 or 11 a.m.
Guten Tag!Good day! / Hello!During the day. The safe, polite default
Guten Abend!Good evening!From about 6 p.m.
Gute Nacht!Good night!Only when someone is going to bed. It is a goodbye, never a greeting

In relaxed situations people skip all of that and say Hallo! (hello!), which works with almost anyone. Among friends you'll also hear Hi!, and in some regions you will hear local greetings:

Regional and informal greetings

GermanEnglishWhere / with whom
Servus!Hi! / Bye!Southern Germany and Austria, informal, works for hello and goodbye
Grüß dich!Hi! (literally "greet you")Informal, with one person you say du to
Grüß Gott!Hello! (literally "greet God")Bavaria and Austria, polite, used with anyone
Moin!Hi!Northern Germany, any time of day
Herzlich willkommen!A warm welcome!When you welcome a guest

Common mistake: saying Gute Nacht when you arrive

English speakers sometimes greet people in the evening with "good night". In German, Gute Nacht only ever means "sleep well" as you part for the night. Walking into a restaurant at 9 p.m., you say Guten Abend.

3. Wie geht's? — asking how someone is

The full question is Wie geht es dir? (How are you? — informal) or Wie geht es Ihnen? (How are you? — formal). In speech it shrinks to Wie geht's? (How's it going?). Literally it means "How does it go for you?", which is why the answer starts with mir (for me).

Answers, from best to worst

GermanEnglish
Sehr gut, danke!Very well, thanks!
Mir geht es gut, danke.I'm well, thanks.
Gut, danke. Und dir? / Und Ihnen?Fine, thanks. And you? (informal / formal)
Auch gut.Fine too.
Es geht. / Es geht so.It's okay. / So-so.
Nicht so gut.Not so good.
Schlecht.Bad.

Common mistake: Ich bin gut

Translating "I am good" word for word gives Ich bin gut, which describes your skill or character ("I am good at it", "I am a good person"), not how you feel. For wellbeing, always use Mir geht es gut. Learn it as one fixed chunk; the grammar behind mir comes in Chapter 19.

4. Alltagsphrasen (everyday phrases)

These tiny words carry a huge amount of daily life. Germans use them constantly, and using them well makes you sound polite immediately.

The essential phrases

GermanEnglishNote

Danke. / Danke schön. / Vielen Dank.Thanks. / Thank you very much. / Many thanks.Increasing warmth
Gern geschehen. / Gerne.You're welcome. / My pleasure.Reply to danke
Entschuldigung.Excuse me. / Sorry.To get attention or apologise for something small

Kein Problem.No problem.
Ja. / Nein.Yes. / No.
Okay. / In Ordnung.Okay. / Alright.
Alles klar.All clear. / Got it.Very common in speech

Entschuldigung vs Es tut mir leid

Both translate as "sorry", but they aren't the same. Entschuldigung is for small things: stepping past someone, bumping an elbow, or stopping a stranger to ask a question (Entschuldigung, wo ist der Bahnhof? — Excuse me, where is the train station?). Es tut mir leid expresses real regret or sympathy: you are late, you broke something, or someone has bad news. Using Entschuldigung for serious news sounds careless.

5. Sich vorstellen (introducing yourself)

A self-introduction is built from a handful of short sentences. Each uses a verb you will conjugate properly in Chapter 7; for now, learn the ich (I) form so you can talk about yourself, and the du and Sie forms so you can ask others.

The building blocks

TopicSay it about yourselfAsk (du)Ask (Sie)
NameIch heiße Anna.Wie heißt du?Wie heißen Sie?
OriginIch komme aus Uganda.Woher kommst du?Woher kommen Sie?
HomeIch wohne in Kampala.Wo wohnst du?Wo wohnen Sie?
AgeIch bin 25 Jahre alt.Wie alt bist du?Wie alt sind Sie?
JobIch arbeite als Lehrerin.Was machst du beruflich?Was machen Sie beruflich?
LanguagesIch spreche Englisch und ein bisschen Deutsch.Welche Sprachen sprichst du?Welche Sprachen sprechen Sie?
HobbyIch lese gern.Was sind deine Hobbys?Was sind Ihre Hobbys?

Translations: my name is Anna / I come from Uganda / I live in Kampala / I am 25 years old / I work as a teacher / I speak English and a little German / I like reading.

There are three ways to give your name. Ich heiße Anna. (literally "I am called Anna") is the most common. Ich bin Anna. (I'm Anna.) is relaxed and friendly. Mein Name ist Anna Schmidt. (My name is Anna Schmidt.) is more formal and usually includes your surname.

aus and in: two small words, one rule

Where from vs where

Woher? (where from?) is answered with aus (from): Ich komme aus Kenia. (I come from Kenya.)

Wo? (where?) is answered with in (in): Ich wohne in Nairobi. (I live in Nairobi.)

Most countries are used without an article: aus Deutschland, aus Uganda, aus Italien. A small group of countries always carries an article, and the article changes after aus and in (because these words take the dative case, taught in Chapter 10). Learn these as fixed phrases now:

Countries that need an article

CountryI come from ...I live in ...
die Türkei (Turkey)aus der Türkeiin der Türkei
die Schweiz (Switzerland)aus der Schweizin der Schweiz
der Iran (Iran)aus dem Iranim Iran
der Sudan (Sudan)aus dem Sudanim Sudan
die USA (the USA, plural)aus den USAin den USA
die Niederlande (the Netherlands, plural)aus den Niederlandenin den Niederlanden

im is simply a short form of in dem.

Common mistakes with origin and age

  • "Ich wohne aus Berlin" is wrong. Living goes with in: Ich wohne in Berlin.
  • "Ich habe 25 Jahre" copies Spanish or French. German uses sein (to be): Ich bin 25 Jahre alt, or simply Ich bin 25.
  • "Ich bin eine Lehrerin" is wrong. Jobs take no article: Ich bin Lehrerin. (I am a teacher.)

6. Jemanden kennenlernen (getting to know someone)

Once names are exchanged, conversation moves to open questions. The tip on the topic sheet is a good one: ask questions that need more than yes or no, and listen for whether people use Herr (Mr) and Frau (Mrs / Ms), because that tells you to stay with Sie.

Getting-to-know-you questions

GermanEnglish
Was machst du in deiner Freizeit?What do you do in your free time?
Was sind deine Hobbys?What are your hobbies?
Magst du Musik / Sport / Filme?Do you like music / sport / films?
Seit wann lernst du Deutsch?How long have you been learning German?
Bist du verheiratet?Are you married?
Hast du Kinder?Do you have children?
Wie ist deine Telefonnummer?What is your phone number?

And the answers you'll need about your free time: Ich lese gern. (I like reading.) Ich spiele Fußball. (I play football.) Ich höre gern Musik. (I like listening to music.) Ich treffe gern Freunde. (I like meeting friends.) Putting gern (gladly) after the verb is how German says "like doing something".

When you meet someone new, you finish with Freut mich! (Pleased to meet you!), or the fuller Schön, dich / Sie kennenzulernen. (Nice to meet you.) The reply is Mich auch! (Me too!) or Ganz meinerseits. (The pleasure is mine.)

7. Jemanden vorstellen (introducing someone else)

Introducing another person needs only one phrase: Das ist ... (This is ...). It stays the same for a man, a woman or a child, which makes it very easy.

Introducing others

GermanEnglishSituation
Das ist Tom.This is Tom.Informal
Das ist mein Freund Tom.This is my friend Tom.Male friend
Das ist meine Freundin Lisa.This is my friend Lisa.Female friend
Das ist Frau Müller.This is Mrs Müller.Formal, surname only
Das ist Herr Braun.This is Mr Braun.Formal, surname only
Ich möchte Ihnen Herrn Braun vorstellen.I'd like to introduce Mr Braun to you.Very formal, at work

mein Freund can mean more than "friend"

mein Freund / meine Freundin can mean "my friend", but very often means "my boyfriend" / "my girlfriend". To make clear someone is just a friend, Germans say ein Freund von mir / eine Freundin von mir (a friend of mine). And Herr and Frau go with the surname only: Frau Müller, never "Frau Anna".

8. Verabschiedungen (saying goodbye)

Goodbyes

GermanEnglishTone
Auf Wiedersehen!Goodbye!Formal, the safe choice
Auf Wiederhören!Goodbye! (on the phone)Formal; literally "until we hear each other again"
Tschüss!Bye!Informal, very common
Bis bald!See you soon!Friendly
Bis später!See you later!Later the same day

Schönen Tag noch!Have a nice day!Very common in shops

Culture: what people actually do

In formal situations Germans shake hands when they meet and often again when they leave, with a firm handshake and eye contact. Friends may hug. When you enter a small shop, a doctor's waiting room or a lift, it is normal and polite to say Guten Tag to everyone, and Auf Wiedersehen when you leave.

10. How this appears in the A1 exam

Part 1 of the speaking exam asks you to introduce yourself using key words on a card: Name, Alter, Land, Wohnort, Sprachen, Beruf, Hobby (name, age, country, place you live, languages, job, hobby). The examiner may then ask you to spell your name or say a phone number (Chapters 2 and 3). If you can say one clear sentence for each key word, you have that part.
