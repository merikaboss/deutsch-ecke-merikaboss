Das Alphabet — Aussprache

Pronunciation: how letters actually sound inside real words

Original topic sheet View "Das Alphabet – Aussprache" in the Picture Book →

Chapter 3 taught you what to call a letter when you spell something. This chapter is about what that letter actually sounds like when it's sitting inside a normal word — a completely different (and more useful) skill.

Vowels: short vs. long

Every German vowel (a, e, i, o, u) has a short version and a long version, and the difference actually changes the meaning of words. As a rough guide: a vowel is usually long when followed by a single consonant or an h, and short when followed by a double consonant.

VowelShort exampleLong example
aStadt (city)Staat (state/country)
eBett (bed)Beet (flower bed)
iMitte (middle)Miete (rent)
oSonne (sun)Sohn (son)
uMutter (mother)Mut (courage)

⚠️ Common mistake: ignoring vowel length

English speakers often flatten every vowel to roughly the same length, because English doesn't use vowel length to distinguish word meaning the way German does. To German ears, mixing up Staat and Stadt is as different as "sheep" and "ship" are to English ears — worth the extra attention.

💬 Example: the classic confusion

wir (we) sounds like "veer", while vier (four) sounds like "feer." Two totally different words that an English speaker's instincts would pronounce backwards from how a German would.

The letter combinations sch, ch, sp, st

CombinationSoundExample
schEnglish "sh"Schule (school) → "SHOO-le"
sp-, st-"shp-", "sht-" at the start of a wordSprache (language) → "SHPRAH-khe", Straße (street) → "SHTRAH-se"
chtwo different sounds — see belowich (I), Buch (book)

📐 Grammar rule: the two "ch" sounds

ch is soft (like a hissed "hy," made at the front of the mouth — the ich-Laut) after i, e, ä, ö, ü and consonants: ich, nicht (not). It's harsh (like clearing your throat gently, made at the back — the ach-Laut) after a, o, u, au: Buch, auch (also). Neither sound exists in English, so both take practice — listening to native audio helps far more than reading a description.

The umlauts and how they change meaning

Because umlauts are separate sounds, adding or removing one changes the word entirely — often turning singular into plural, or one word into a completely different one.

💬 Examples

  • Mutter (mother) → Mütter (mothers)
  • Bruder (brother) → Brüder (brothers)
  • schon (already) vs. schön (beautiful) — completely different words

The final -e and the letter r

A word-final -e is always pronounced as a light "uh" sound, never silent as it often is in English: Tasse (cup) is "TAH-suh," not "tass." The letter r is usually a soft, throaty sound rather than the English tongue-curl "r" — at the end of a word or syllable it often nearly disappears into an "ah" sound, e.g. Mutter ending closer to "MOO-tah" than "MOO-ter."

Exercises

✏️ Try it yourself

1. Say these two words out loud and explain the difference in meaning: Stadt vs. Staat.
2. How is the w in Wasser pronounced? How is the v in Vater pronounced?
3. Which "ch" sound (soft/ich-Laut or harsh/ach-Laut) appears in ich? Which one appears in Buch?
4. Why do schon and schön sound different, and what does each mean?
5. Read this sentence out loud, paying attention to every rule above: "Mein Vater wohnt in der Stadt und spricht sehr schnell."
