Das Alphabet – Aussprache

How German really sounds: vowels, umlauts, tricky consonants, the R, and word stress

Original topic sheet View "Das Alphabet – Aussprache" in the Picture Book →

Here is some genuinely good news: German spelling is far more regular than English. In English, "ough" sounds different in though, through, tough and cough. In German, once you know the rules, you can read almost any word aloud correctly the first time you see it, even if you don't know what it means. This chapter gives you those rules. Read every example out loud. Your mouth has to learn this, not just your eyes.

1. Vowels: long or short?

Every German vowel has a long and a short version, and the difference can change the meaning of a word. English speakers tend to ignore vowel length, so this is the first thing to train. Luckily, the spelling tells you which one it is.

How to tell from the spelling

The vowel is long when...Example
it is doubled (aa, ee, oo)der Staat (state), der Tee (tea), das Boot (boat)
it is followed by a silent hder Sohn (son), die Uhr (clock), fahren (to drive)
it is iedie Liebe (love), vier (four)
it is followed by only one consonantder Tag (day), gut (good), der Name (name)
The vowel is short when...Example
it is followed by a double consonantdie Mutter (mother), kommen (to come)
it is followed by ck or tzder Zucker (sugar), die Katze (cat)
it is followed by two or more different consonantsdie Stadt (city), das Kind (child)

To German ears, Stadt and Staat are as different as English ship and sheep.

2. The umlauts: ä, ö, ü

Making each sound

SoundHow to make itExamples
äShort: like "e" in bed. Long: like "ai" in air, without the rdie Äpfel (apples), der Bär (bear)
öSay "ay" as in say. Hold your tongue still and round your lips as if saying "oh"das Öl (oil), schön (beautiful)
üSay "ee" as in see. Hold your tongue still and push your lips into a tight "oo" circledie Tür (door), früh (early)

Corrections to the topic sheet

  • The sheet says ü sounds "like 'oo' in moon". That describes u, not ü. Say Tür with "oo" and you'll say Tour (tour). The tongue must stay in the "ee" position.
  • The sheet says "German has no silent letters". Most letters are read, but h after a vowel is silent: it only makes the vowel long, as in gehen (to go) = "GAY-en".
  • The example word "Pfert" is a typo. It is das Pferd (horse). It does sound like it ends in t, and section 5 explains why.

3. Vowel pairs: ei, ie, eu, äu, au

Diphthongs

SpellingSounds likeExamples
ei (also ai)"eye"mein (my), zwei (two), der Mai (May)

eu / äu"oy" as in toyDeutsch (German), die Häuser (houses)
au"ow" as in howdas Haus (house), auch (also)

ei or ie? Follow the second letter

English speakers confuse these constantly. The trick: say the English name of the second letter. In ei the second letter is i, named "eye", so it sounds "eye". In ie the second letter is e, named "ee", so it sounds "ee".

It matters: Wien (Vienna) vs Wein (wine); die Biene (bee) vs die Beine (legs).

The classic confusion

wir (we) sounds like "veer", and vier (four) sounds like "feer". An English speaker's instinct says both the other way round. Practise: Wir sind vier. (We are four / There are four of us.) = "veer zint feer".

5. Letters that change at the end of a word

Hardening at the end: b → p, d → t, g → k

At the end of a word or syllable, German pronounces b, d, g as their hard partners p, t, k. The spelling does not change, only the sound.

WrittenSounds likeBut in the plural
der Hund (dog)"hoont"die Hunde — "HOON-de", a real d again
das Kind (child)"kint"die Kinder — d
gelb (yellow)"gelp"gelbe — b
der Tag (day)"tahk"die Tage — g

A related rule: -ig at the end of a word sounds like -ich: zwanzig "TSVAN-tsikh", der König (king).

The two ch sounds

Neither exists in English, and the letter before ch decides which one you use.

AfterSoundHowExamples
a, o, u, au"ach" soundA scrape at the back of the throat, as in Scottish lochdas Buch (book), acht, auch
e, i, ä, ö, ü, ei, eu, consonants"ich" soundSay "h" in huge and breathe it harder, at the front of the mouthich (I), nicht (not), die Milch (milk)

You can hear both in one family: das Buch (ach) but die Bücher (books) (ich), because the vowel before changed from u to ü.

Don't replace ich with "ish" or "ik"

English speakers often say ich as "ish" or "ik". "Ish" is close enough to be understood, but "ik" sounds like a different dialect. Aim for a soft hiss, like a cat's warning, with your tongue high behind your bottom teeth.

7. The German R and the -er ending

The standard German R is made at the back of the throat, a light gargle, rather than with the tip of the tongue as in English. The topic sheet's tip, "roll slightly in the throat", is a good description. Some southern speakers roll it with the tongue instead, and both are understood.

When R almost disappears

After a vowel, and especially in the ending -er, the R softens into a short "ah" sound:

  • die Mutter (mother) → "MUT-tah"
  • der Lehrer (teacher) → "LAY-rah"
  • hier (here) → "heeah"

And the final -e is never silent as in English "make". It is a short, relaxed "uh": die Tasse (cup) = "TAS-suh", danke (thanks) = "DAN-kuh".

8. Word stress: which syllable is strong?

Four stress rules

  1. Most German words stress the first syllable: AR-beit (work), MUT-ter, FEN-ster.
  2. These prefixes are never stressed: be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-, emp-. The stress moves to the stem: be-ZAH-len (to pay), ver-STE-hen (to understand), ge-SUND (healthy).
  3. Separable prefixes are always stressed: AN-kommen (to arrive), AUF-stehen (to get up). More in Chapter 21.
  4. Many borrowed words stress the end: Stu-DENT, U-ni-ver-si-TÄT, In-for-ma-TION, and every verb ending in -ieren: stu-DIE-ren (to study).

9. Tips for practising alone

What actually works

  • Listen, then copy. Short videos or podcasts made for learners, and the audio buttons in free online dictionaries, let you hear each word. Pause and repeat.
  • Record yourself on your phone and compare it with the original. You'll hear mistakes you can't notice while speaking.
  • Exaggerate at first. Make the ü rounder and the long vowels longer than feels natural. You can relax later.
  • Use tongue twisters, Zungenbrecher (tongue-breakers), for the hard sounds. There are two in the exercises.
