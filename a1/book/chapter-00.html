How to Use This Book

What A1 is, how the book is built, and how to study from it alone

Original topic sheet View the syllabus map in the Picture Book →

These are my own A1 notes, written out properly so that a friend with no German teacher, no classroom and no translator can still learn from them. That shapes how you should use the book: slowly, one chapter at a time, actually writing the exercises out rather than reading past them. Read this chapter once before you begin. It will save you a lot of confusion later.

1. What "A1" actually means

Language levels in Europe follow a shared scale called the Gemeinsamer Europäischer Referenzrahmen (Common European Framework of Reference, usually shortened to CEFR or GER). It runs from A1 up to C2:

The six levels

LevelNameRoughly means
A1Anfänger (beginner)You can handle simple, familiar everyday situations
A2Grundlegende Kenntnisse (elementary)Routine tasks, simple descriptions of your life
B1Fortgeschrittene Sprachverwendung (intermediate)Travel, work and study situations on your own
B2Selbstständige Sprachverwendung (upper intermediate)Fluent conversation with native speakers
C1–C2Kompetente Sprachverwendung (advanced to mastery)Near-native, academic and professional German

At the end of A1 you should be able to introduce yourself and others, ask and answer simple personal questions (where you live, who you know, what you have), understand slow and clear everyday speech, fill in a form, write a short message, and get through basic situations like shopping, ordering food, asking the way, making an appointment or seeing a doctor. That is exactly what the 54 chapters of this book cover.

2. The official A1 exam

If you want a certificate, the best-known A1 exam is the Goethe-Zertifikat A1: Start Deutsch 1 (Goethe Certificate A1: Start German 1) from the Goethe-Institut. It is often required for a spouse or family visa to Germany. Knowing its shape now helps, because many exercises in this book are built in the same formats.

The four parts of the exam

PartTimeWhat you do
Hören (listening)about 20 minShort everyday conversations, phone messages and announcements, then answer questions
Lesen (reading)25 minShort notes, adverts, signs and notices, then answer questions
Schreiben (writing)20 minFill in a simple form, then write a short personal message
Sprechen (speaking)about 15 min, in a group1: introduce yourself. 2: ask for and give information. 3: make and answer requests

The exam is worth 100 points and you pass with 60. There is no minimum per section, but you cannot retake just one part: below 60 overall means sitting the whole exam again.

4. The one big idea: the four cases

The biggest difference between German and English is the Kasus (case) system. In English, "the" never changes. In German, the word for "the" changes depending on the job a noun is doing in the sentence. There are four jobs, called the four cases:

The four cases at a glance

CaseJob in the sentenceQuestion it answersExample
NominativThe subject: who does itWer? (who?)Der Mann liest. (The man reads.)
AkkusativThe direct object: what is acted onWen? Was? (whom? what?)Ich sehe den Mann. (I see the man.)
DativThe indirect object: who receives or benefitsWem? (to whom?)Ich helfe dem Mann. (I help the man.)
GenitivPossession: whoseWessen? (whose?)das Auto des Mannes (the man's car)

Don't try to learn this table now. Chapter 10 teaches it properly, and every chapter about articles, possessives or pronouns after that gives you the full four-case table for that word type. By the end of the book you will have seen the same four-case pattern so often that it stops feeling foreign.

5. How translations work here

You have no dictionary and no teacher beside you, so every German word or phrase used in an explanation has its English meaning right next to it in brackets, like this: Hallo (hello).

The one exception is the exercises. An exercise is where you do the work, so translating it would simply hand you the answer. If you get stuck, go back and reread the explanation above it rather than guessing.

7. How to study from this book alone

A method that works

  1. Look first. Open the chapter's sheet in the Picture Book and take in the overview.
  2. Read the chapter slowly, saying every German example out loud. Chapter 4 teaches you how to pronounce them.
  3. Learn every noun with its article. Never learn Tisch (table), always der Tisch. The article is part of the word.
  4. Write the exercises out by hand, then check them against the answers. Mark what you got wrong and why.
  5. Come back after two days and redo only the exercises you got wrong. This spacing is what moves words into long-term memory.
  6. Make one real sentence about your own life with the new grammar before moving on.

A realistic pace is two or three chapters a week, which gets you through A1 in about four to five months of steady work. Short daily sessions of twenty to thirty minutes beat one long session on a Sunday.

The most common way self-learners stall

Reading a chapter, understanding it, and moving on without writing anything. Understanding feels like learning, but it isn't. Recognising dem in a table is easy; producing it yourself in the middle of a sentence is the actual skill, and only practice builds it.

Übungen (Exercises)

Before you start

1. Which four skills does the A1 exam test? Write their German names.
2. How many points out of 100 do you need to pass?
3. Match each case to its question word: Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ, Genitiv — wem? / wessen? / wer? / wen?
4. Why does this book not translate the exercises?
5. Write your own study plan: which days and times will you study, and how many chapters per week?
