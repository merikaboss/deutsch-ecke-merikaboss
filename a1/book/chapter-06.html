Personalpronomen (ich, du, wir...)

The three persons, du / ihr / Sie, the many jobs of sie and es, man — and all pronouns in all four cases

Original topic sheet View "Personalpronomen (1., 2., 3. Person)" in the Picture Book →

A Personalpronomen (personal pronoun) is a short word that stands in for a person or a thing: I, you, he, she, it, we, they. Pronouns matter far more in German than their size suggests, because the verb changes its ending to match them. You can't conjugate a single verb correctly (Chapter 7) until you know exactly which pronoun you're using. This chapter teaches them thoroughly, including the German distinctions English doesn't make.

1. The three persons

Grammar sorts pronouns into three Personen (persons), each with a singular and a plural:

The subject pronouns (Nominativ)

PersonWho it isSingularPlural
1st — erste Person The speaker ich (I) wir (we)
2nd — zweite Person The person spoken to du (you, informal) ihr (you all, informal)
3rd — dritte Person Anyone or anything else er (he / it), sie (she / it), es (it) sie (they)
Formal The person or people spoken to, politely Sie (you — one person or many, always with a capital S)

To see how much the verb depends on the pronoun, look at sein (to be), the most important verb in the language. Every form is different, just as English has am, are, is:

Each pronoun with sein

ich binI amwir sindwe are
du bistyou areihr seidyou (all) are

Sie sindyou are (formal)

ich is written small

English capitalises "I" everywhere. German writes ich with a small letter unless it begins a sentence: Heute bin ich müde. (I am tired today.) The formal Sie is the only pronoun that is always capitalised.

2. Choosing "you": du, ihr or Sie

Two questions decide it

InformalFormal
One personduSie
Two or more peopleihrSie

1. How many people am I talking to? 2. Is the relationship informal or formal? Formal Sie doesn't change for number: one stranger or a room full of strangers is still Sie.

Who gets which

du / ihr — informalSie — formal
Family and relatives, including older onesStrangers and adults you've just met in a formal setting
Friends, partners, children and teenagersShop staff, waiters, officials, police, bank staff
Classmates and, in many workplaces, colleaguesDoctors, your boss, a landlord, a job interviewer
Animals, and God in prayerOlder people you don't know well
Most social media and chat with people your ageFormal letters and emails

Moving from Sie to du is a small social step. The verbs for it are siezen (to say "Sie") and duzen (to say "du"). Traditionally the older person, or the person in the higher position, offers it: Wollen wir uns duzen? (Shall we say "du" to each other?) Until that happens, keep Sie.

Common mistakes with "you"

  • Using du with a group. Talking to two friends: Kommt ihr mit? (Are you coming along?), not "Kommst du mit?"
  • Switching in the middle. Starting with Sie and slipping into du sounds careless. Choose one and keep it.
  • Forgetting the verb. Sie takes the same verb form as "they": Sie sind, Sie haben, Sie kommen — never "Sie ist" for "you are".

3. One spelling, three meanings: sie

The word sie / Sie can mean she, they or formal you. In writing, two clues always tell you which: the capital letter and the verb ending.

Decoding sie

You seeClueMeaning
sie kommtsmall s + verb ending -t (singular)she comes
sie kommensmall s + verb ending -en (plural)they come
Sie kommen (mid-sentence)capital S + -enyou come (formal)

At the start of a sentence every word has a capital, so Sie kommen aus Uganda could mean "They come from Uganda" or "You come from Uganda". Only context decides. In speech, the situation always makes it clear.

4. er, sie, es for things

In English, every object is "it". In German, every noun has a grammatical gender (der / die / das, Chapter 10), and the pronoun follows the noun's gender, not whether it is alive:

The pronoun matches the article

ArticlePronounExample
dererDer Tisch ist groß. Er ist braun. (The table is big. It is brown.)
diesieDie Lampe ist alt. Sie ist kaputt. (The lamp is old. It is broken.)
dasesDas Auto ist neu. Es ist schnell. (The car is new. It is fast.)
die (plural)sieDie Schuhe sind teuer. Sie kosten 90 Euro. (The shoes are expensive. They cost 90 euros.)

Two gender traps

  • Using es for every "it". Wo ist der Schlüssel? — Er ist hier. (Where is the key? — It's here.) Because Schlüssel is masculine, "it" is er, not es.
  • People whose noun is neuter. das Mädchen (the girl) and das Kind (the child) are grammatically neuter, so grammar books say Das Mädchen ist nett. Es ist zehn. In everyday speech many people say sie for a girl, especially once they know her name. Both are heard; in writing, es is the grammatical choice.

5. es with no "it" at all

es also works as an empty subject, where there is no real "it", because a German sentence needs something in the subject slot. You will use these fixed expressions constantly:

The impersonal es

GermanEnglish
Es regnet. / Es schneit.It's raining. / It's snowing.
Es ist kalt.It's cold.
Es ist acht Uhr.It's eight o'clock.
Wie geht es dir?How are you?
Es gibt einen Supermarkt hier.There is a supermarket here.
Es tut mir leid.I'm sorry.

es gibt (there is / there are) is especially useful. It never changes for plural: Es gibt drei Supermärkte. (There are three supermarkets.)

6. man: "one", "people", "you" in general

The general pronoun

man means people in general, like English "you", "one" or "they" when no specific person is meant. It takes the same verb form as er:

  • In Österreich spricht man Deutsch. (In Austria people speak German.)
  • Wie sagt man das auf Deutsch? (How do you say that in German?)
  • Hier darf man nicht rauchen. (You may not smoke here.)

You'll see man on signs and in instructions all the time. Don't confuse it with der Mann (the man), which has a capital letter and two n's.

7. All personal pronouns in all four cases

So far every pronoun has been the subject, which is the Nominativ. But pronouns change form depending on their job in the sentence, just like English I / me, he / him, we / us. German has four cases (introduced in Chapter 0, explained fully in Chapter 10), so here is the complete table. English uses "him" for both of the last two. German separates them: some verbs take the accusative (sehen to see, anrufen to call) and others the dative (helfen to help, danken to thank). Chapter 19 gives you the lists.

About the genitive pronouns

The genitive column is complete for reference, but these forms are rare today. They appear in formal or old-fashioned language with a few verbs and expressions, for example Gedenke meiner! (Remember me!). They do not mean "my" or "your". To show possession you use the possessive articles mein, dein, sein, ihr (my, your, his, her), which have their own full four-case chapter (Chapter 14). Also notice that ihr has several jobs: ihr (you all), ihr (to her), and the possessive ihr (her / their).

3. Ergänzen Sie: Wo ist die Tasche? — ___ ist im Auto.
