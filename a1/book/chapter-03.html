Das Alphabet – Buchstaben

The letters and their names, umlauts and ß, capital letters, and spelling words aloud

Original topic sheet View "Das Alphabet – Buchstaben" in the Picture Book →

In Germany you will spell things out loud far more often than you might expect: your surname at the doctor's reception, your email address on the phone, the name of your street for a delivery, a car number plate. This chapter teaches the names of the letters (what you say when you spell) and the written rules around them. Chapter 4 then teaches how letters sound inside words, which is a separate skill.

1. The 26 letters and their names

German uses the same 26 letters as English, but most of them have different names. Read the table out loud slowly, twice. Don't read it silently: the whole point is the sound.

The three letters everyone gets wrong

  • E and I swap. German E is named "ay" and German I is named "ee". So when a German spells I-N-A, you hear "ee-en-ah". Almost every English speaker mixes these up at first.
  • V is "fau". The topic sheet shows it as "ve", but that is a slip: its German name is fau. The letter W takes the name "vay" instead.
  • J is "yot", not "jay", and it is pronounced like English Y: ja (yes) sounds like "yah".

2. The extra letters: Ä, Ö, Ü and ß

German adds four characters to the alphabet. The two dots are called an Umlaut. They are not decoration: they change the sound completely and can change a word's meaning.

The umlauts

LetterNameHow to make the sound
Ä ää ("a-Umlaut")Like the "e" in bed, or the "ai" in air when long
Ö öö ("o-Umlaut")No English sound. Say "ay", then round your lips as if for "oh" without moving your tongue
Ü üü ("u-Umlaut")No English sound. Say "ee", then round your lips tightly as if for "oo"

That last pair shows something you will meet often: an umlaut is one of the ways German forms plurals.

ß — Eszett or scharfes S (sharp S)

ß is one letter, pronounced as a clear "ss" as in hiss. It is not a B. It is written only after a long vowel or a vowel pair like ei or au. After a short vowel, German writes ss instead:

Long vowel → ßShort vowel → ss
die Straße (street)die Tasse (cup)
groß (big)das Wasser (water)
heißen (to be called)wissen (to know)
der Fuß (foot)der Fluss (river)

ß had no capital form for a long time, so in all-capital text it became SS (STRASSE). Since 2017 an official capital ẞ exists and is allowed, but SS is still more common. Switzerland does not use ß at all and always writes ss.

When you have no umlaut key

If your keyboard, an online form or an email address can't take these letters, German has an accepted replacement: write an e after the vowel, and write ss for ß.

Substitutes

This is why a Mr Müller's email is usually mueller. It's a fallback, not the normal spelling.

3. Capital letters: a rule English doesn't have

Großschreibung (capitalisation)

German writes a capital letter for:

  • every noun, not just names: der Tisch, die Stadt, das Wasser, die Liebe (the table, the city, the water, love)
  • the first word of a sentence
  • names of people, places and countries
  • the formal Sie (you) and its forms Ihr, Ihnen

Adjectives, verbs and the word ich (I) are written small, unless they start a sentence. Languages and nationalities as adjectives are also small: deutsch, but the language as a noun is Deutsch.

See the rule in one sentence

Ich trinke am Morgen einen Kaffee in der Küche. (I drink a coffee in the kitchen in the morning.)

Capitals: Ich (start), Morgen, Kaffee, Küche (nouns). Small: trinke (verb), am, einen, in, der (small words).

Capital letters can change meaning

Sie with a capital is formal "you"; sie in the middle of a sentence means "she" or "they". Kommen Sie morgen? (Are you coming tomorrow?) is not the same as Kommen sie morgen? (Are they coming tomorrow?). In an email to an office, a small sie is a real mistake.

The spelling alphabet: "A wie Anton"

On a bad phone line, letters like B, D, P and T sound alike. Germans solve this by adding a word: M wie Martha (M as in Martha). The traditional names below are still what you will hear most. In 2022 an official version with city names was introduced (A wie Aachen, B wie Berlin), so you may hear those too.

5. Alphabetical order

In dictionaries and lists, umlauts are sorted as if they had no dots: Äpfel (apples) sits among the A words, and Müller sits right after Muller. ß is sorted as ss. So if you can't find a word under Ü, look under U.

Übungen (Exercises)

Teil 1 — Buchstaben erkennen

1. Which letters are these? Write them: ay · ee · fau · yot · vay · tset
2. Say these letter names aloud, then write the English sound for each: E, I, V, W, J, Z
3. Welches Wort ist das? hah – ah – en – day – ay – el
4. Welches Wort ist das? es – tsay – ha – oh – en (Tipp: think of the umlaut pair on this page)

Teil 2 — ß oder ss? Groß oder klein?

5. Ergänzen Sie ß oder ss: die Stra__e · das Wa__er · gro__ · die Ta__e · hei__en
6. Schreiben Sie ohne Umlaut, wie in einer E-Mail-Adresse: Jürgen Schäfer · Björn Weiß
7. Schreiben Sie richtig groß und klein: ich wohne mit meiner familie in einer kleinen wohnung in der stadt.
8. Welche Bedeutung? „Können Sie mir helfen?" — is Sie "she", "they" or formal "you"? How do you know?

Teil 3 — Fehler finden

9. „Mein name ist Anna und ich komme aus kenia."
10. „Ich wohne in der Strase 12."
11. Someone spells „V – A – T – E – R" and says the V as "vay". What letter did they really say, and what is the correct name for V?

Teil 4 — Am Telefon (exam format: speaking and writing)

12. Buchstabieren Sie Ihren Vornamen und Nachnamen laut, mit der Buchstabiertafel: „K wie Kaufmann, ..."
13. Schreiben Sie diese E-Mail-Adresse: „peter unterstrich braun at gmx punkt de"
14. Sagen Sie diese Adresse laut, Wort für Wort: s.okello-mayer@web.de
15. Schreiben Sie einen Dialog (6 Zeilen): Sie rufen bei einer Firma an. Die Sekretärin fragt nach Ihrem Namen und Ihrer E-Mail-Adresse. Sie buchstabieren beides.
