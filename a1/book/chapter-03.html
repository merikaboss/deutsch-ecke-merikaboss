Das Alphabet — Buchstaben

The alphabet: letters, their names, and spelling words aloud

Original topic sheet View "Das Alphabet – Buchstaben" in the Picture Book →

The German alphabet uses the same 26 letters as English, plus four extra characters: three umlauts (Ä, Ö, Ü) and one special letter, ß (Eszett / scharfes S — "sharp S"). This chapter is about naming the letters — what you say when you spell a word out loud. Chapter 4 covers how letters actually sound inside real words, which is a different (and more useful) skill.

Why this matters

Germans spell things out loud constantly — on the phone giving an email address, confirming a surname at a doctor's office, reading out a license-plate number. Knowing the letter names lets you both do this and understand it when it's done to you.

⚠️ Common mistake: reading letter names as if they were English

The German letter names look similar to English ones on paper but sound quite different — E is named "eh" (not "ee" like the English letter E), while I is named "ih" (closer to the English letter E!). This swap trips up almost everyone at first. Say them out loud, don't just read them silently.

The umlauts: Ä, Ö, Ü

An umlaut is the two dots over a vowel. It's not decoration — it's a completely different sound, and a completely different letter name.

LetterGerman nameHow it sounds
Ä äähLike the "e" in "bed"
Ö ööhNo English equivalent — round your lips as if for "o," but try to say "e"
Ü üühNo English equivalent — round your lips as if for "oo," but try to say "ee"

If a keyboard genuinely can't type umlauts, German has an accepted substitute: write an extra e after the vowel — ä → ae, ö → oe, ü → ue. So schön (beautiful) can be written schoen if you have no other option. This is a fallback, not the normal way to write.

The letter ß (Eszett)

ß is not a "weird B" — it's a single letter that represents a sharp "s" sound (like the "ss" in "hiss"). It only ever appears in lowercase, and only after a long vowel or diphthong. Its fallback substitute is ss: Straße (street) can be written Strasse if needed (this is actually the standard spelling in Switzerland, which doesn't use ß at all).

Spelling a word out loud

💬 Example — spelling your name on the phone

A: Wie schreibt man Ihren Namen?
A: How do you write your name?

B: Müller. M-Ü-L-L-E-R.
B: Müller. Spelled: em, üh, el, el, eh, er.

You could also be asked directly: Können Sie das bitte buchstabieren? (Could you please spell that out?) — the verb buchstabieren means "to spell".

Exercises

✏️ Try it yourself

1. Spell your own first name using German letter names (write it out, e.g. "J-A-M-E-S" → "yot, ah, em, eh, es").
2. What is the German name for the letter E? What is the German name for the letter I? Why do learners often confuse these two?
3. Write the umlaut-free fallback spelling for: Grüße, schön, können.
4. What sound does ß represent, and what two letters can replace it if you can't type it?
5. Someone says to you: "Können Sie das bitte buchstabieren?" — what are they asking you to do?
