Krank sein / Beim Arzt

Being ill, and a visit to the doctor

Original topic sheet View "Krank Sein" in the Picture Book →

This is one of the chapters you will actually need, probably sooner than you would like. Being ill in a foreign country is stressful, and the language you need is very specific: you have to say what is wrong, point at the part of you that hurts, understand what a doctor asks you, and follow the advice you are given. The good news is that German handles illness with a small number of fixed patterns. Learn those patterns and you can describe almost any symptom.

1. The three ways to say you are ill

German gives you three different sentence shapes, and beginners usually learn one and use it for everything. They are not interchangeable, so it is worth separating them from the start.

The three patterns

PatternUsed forExample
sein + krank Your overall state — you are an ill person right now Ich bin krank. (I am ill.)
haben + illness A specific named illness or symptom you "have" Ich habe Fieber. (I have a fever.)
mir geht es ... How you feel in general — vaguer, very common Mir geht es nicht gut. (I don't feel well.)

The first one uses sein (to be), which you met in Chapter 8. Because krank (ill, sick) is an adjective standing after the verb, it never changes its ending — the same form works for everybody:

krank sein — the full conjugation

GermanEnglish
ich bin krankI am ill
du bist krankyou are ill (informal)
er / sie / es ist krankhe / she / it is ill
wir sind krankwe are ill
ihr seid krankyou are ill (plural, informal)
sie / Sie sind krankthey are ill / you are ill (formal)

2. Mir geht es nicht gut — why it says "mir"

This phrase confuses learners because there is no ich (I) in it anywhere. Literally it says "to me it goes not well." German treats feeling as something that happens to you rather than something you do, so the person goes into the dative case — mir (to me), dir (to you), ihm (to him), ihr (to her).

You have already used this pattern without noticing. Back in Chapter 1 you learned Wie geht es dir? (How are you?) and answered Mir geht es gut. (I'm well.) Being ill is simply the negative of that same sentence, so there is nothing new to memorise:

The same sentence, three strengths

  • Mir geht es gut. (I'm well.)
  • Mir geht es nicht so gut. (I'm not feeling so great.) — mild, polite
  • Mir geht es nicht gut. (I don't feel well.) — clear and direct
  • Mir geht es sehr schlecht. (I feel very bad.) — serious

Two more phrases from the topic sheet belong here: Ich fühle mich schlecht. (I feel bad.) — note the mich (myself), because sich fühlen (to feel) always needs it — and the idiom Ich bin nicht in Form. (I'm not on form / not in shape.), which is lighter and slightly informal.

3. Naming the illness

For a named illness, German uses haben (to have). Learn each illness together with its article, because you will need the gender the moment you want to say "the" or "a" in front of it.

Krankheiten (illnesses)

GermanEnglishIn a sentence
die Erkältunga coldIch habe eine Erkältung.
die Grippethe fluIch habe die Grippe.
der Hustena coughIch habe Husten.
der Schnupfena runny noseIch habe Schnupfen.
das Fiebera feverIch habe Fieber.
die ÜbelkeitnauseaMir ist übel.

Common mistake: adding an article that shouldn't be there

English says "I have a cough." German usually drops the article for symptoms you simply "have": Ich habe Husten, not "Ich habe einen Husten." The same goes for Fieber and Schnupfen. But Erkältung keeps its article (eine Erkältung), and Grippe normally takes die. When in doubt, leave the article out for short symptom words and keep it for the two longer illness names.

4. Saying what hurts — two ways

This is the part that unlocks the whole topic, because it lets you build a sentence about any body part instead of memorising a fixed list. German has two routes, and the topic sheet shows both.

Route A: the -schmerzen compound

der Schmerz (the pain) becomes plural die Schmerzen (the pains), and German glues it straight onto the body part to make one long word. You then simply "have" it:

Body part + schmerzen

Body partCompoundSentence
der Kopf (head)KopfschmerzenIch habe Kopfschmerzen. (I have a headache.)
der Hals (throat, neck)HalsschmerzenIch habe Halsschmerzen. (I have a sore throat.)
der Bauch (stomach, belly)BauchschmerzenIch habe Bauchschmerzen. (I have a stomachache.)
der Rücken (back)RückenschmerzenIch habe Rückenschmerzen. (I have back pain.)
das Ohr (ear)OhrenschmerzenIch habe Ohrenschmerzen. (I have an earache.)
der Zahn (tooth)ZahnschmerzenIch habe Zahnschmerzen. (I have toothache.)

Because the compound is plural, it never takes ein (a). You say Ich habe Kopfschmerzen, never "ein Kopfschmerz." Notice too that Ohr adds -en before joining (Ohren­schmerzen) — German often uses the plural of the body part when there are two of them.

Route B: weh tun

The rule: the body part is the subject

weh tun (to hurt) turns the sentence around. The body part does the action, and the person it happens to goes in the dative — the same mir you saw in section 2:

Mein Kopf tut mir weh. (My head hurts. — literally "my head does pain to me.")

If the body part is plural, the verb becomes tun: Meine Augen tun mir weh. (My eyes hurt.)

Singular vs plural

  • Mein Rücken tut weh. (My back hurts.) — one back, tut
  • Mein Ohr tut weh. (My ear hurts.) — one ear
  • Meine Ohren tun weh. (My ears hurt.) — two ears, tun
  • Meine Zähne tun weh. (My teeth hurt.) — plural of der Zahn

Both routes are correct and native speakers use both. If you want one rule of thumb: use the -schmerzen compound when you are naming a condition to a doctor, and weh tun when you are pointing at something that hurts right now.

5. Giving and understanding advice

When you tell someone you are ill, the reply will almost always be advice, built from the modal verbs you met in Chapter 16. Three carry almost all of it:

The three advice verbs

VerbStrengthExample
sollen (should) A suggestion Du sollst viel schlafen. (You should sleep a lot.)
müssen (must) Necessity Du musst zum Arzt gehen. (You must go to the doctor.)
dürfen (to be allowed) Permission — usually negative here Du darfst nicht arbeiten. (You must not work.)

Remember the word order rule from Chapter 16: the modal verb takes second position and the real verb goes to the very end of the sentence in its basic -en form. In Du musst viel Wasser trinken (You must drink a lot of water), trinken (to drink) waits right at the end.

Common mistake: nicht müssen is not "must not"

English "you must not" is a prohibition, but German Du musst nicht arbeiten means you don't have to work — it removes an obligation. To actually forbid something you need dürfen: Du darfst nicht arbeiten. (You are not allowed to work.) Mixing these two up is the single most consequential error in this chapter.

Advice also comes as a direct command, using the imperative from Chapter 23: Ruh dich aus! (Rest!), Trink viel Wasser! (Drink plenty of water!), Nimm eine Tablette! (Take a tablet!), Geh zum Arzt! (Go to the doctor!)

6. Beim Arzt — at the doctor's

Here is the whole visit as one conversation. Read it twice: once for meaning, once noticing that every sentence uses a pattern from the sections above.

Ein Gespräch beim Arzt (A conversation at the doctor's)

GermanEnglish
Patient: Guten Tag, Herr Doktor.Hello, doctor.
Arzt: Guten Tag. Was fehlt Ihnen?Hello. What's wrong with you?
Patient: Ich habe Halsschmerzen und Fieber.I have a sore throat and a fever.
Arzt: Seit wann sind Sie krank?Since when have you been ill?
Patient: Seit gestern.Since yesterday.
Arzt: Öffnen Sie bitte den Mund.Please open your mouth.
Arzt: Sie haben eine Erkältung.You have a cold.
Arzt: Sie sollen viel trinken und sich ausruhen.You should drink a lot and rest.
Patient: Danke, Herr Doktor.Thank you, doctor.
Arzt: Gute Besserung!Get well soon!

Was fehlt Ihnen? deserves a note. Literally it asks "what is missing to you?" and it is the standard, slightly formal way a doctor opens. A friend would instead ask Was hast du? (What's wrong with you?) or Was ist mit dir? (What's the matter with you?)

Worth knowing before you need it

In Germany, Austria and Switzerland an Apotheke (pharmacy) is not the same as a Drogerie (drugstore). The Drogerie sells shampoo and vitamins; real medicine — even simple painkillers — is sold only in the Apotheke, and much of it needs a Rezept (prescription). If you are employed and stay home ill, your employer will ask for a Krankschreibung (sick note), which only a doctor can issue.

Two short exchanges showing it all working together:

Im Kontext (in context)

A: Du siehst nicht gut aus. (You don't look well.)
B: Ja, ich bin krank. Ich habe Husten und Schnupfen. (Yes, I'm ill. I have a cough and a runny nose.)
A: Was sollst du tun? (What should you do?)
B: Ich soll viel trinken und mich ausruhen. (I should drink a lot and rest.)

A: Wie geht es dir heute? (How are you today?)
B: Besser, danke. Aber mein Kopf tut noch weh. (Better, thanks. But my head still hurts.)
