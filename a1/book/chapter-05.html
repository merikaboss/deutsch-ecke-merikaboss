Satzstruktur, Teil 1 und 2

Sentence structure: the rule that governs almost every German sentence

Original topic sheet View "Satzstruktur und Verben" in the Picture Book →

This is the single most important grammar rule in German — more useful than any vocabulary list. Once it clicks, entire sentences start making sense at a glance.

The verb-second rule (V2)

In a normal statement, the conjugated verb is always the second element of the sentence — not necessarily the second word, but the second logical block of meaning. Whatever comes before the verb can be swapped out, but the verb itself does not move.

Example: same meaning, different starting point

Position 1Verb (position 2)Rest of sentence
Ichlerneheute Deutsch.
Heutelerneich Deutsch.
Deutschlerneich heute.

All three mean roughly "I am learning German today" with a different word emphasized first. Notice lerne (am learning) never moves from position 2, and the subject ich (I) jumps after the verb whenever something else starts the sentence.

Common mistake: keeping the subject first no matter what

English speakers instinctively want to say "Heute ich lerne Deutsch" — subject right after the time word, like in English ("Today I learn German"). German doesn't allow this: whatever isn't the verb's fixed position 2 slot, the subject must move after the verb once something else takes position 1. Say Heute lerne ich Deutsch.

Yes/no questions: the verb jumps to position 1

To turn a statement into a yes/no question, simply move the verb to the very front, with nothing before it:

StatementQuestion
Du lernst Deutsch.Lernst du Deutsch? (Are you learning German?)
Sie kommt aus Berlin.Kommt sie aus Berlin? (Is she from Berlin?)

W-Fragen: questions with a question word

When a question starts with a question word (wer, was, wo, wann, warum, wie — covered fully in Chapter 17), that question word simply occupies position 1, and the verb-second rule still applies exactly as before:

Position 1 (question word)Verb (position 2)Rest
Wowohnstdu?
Warumlernstdu Deutsch?

Negation: where "nicht" goes

nicht (not) doesn't have one fixed spot, but a reliable default is: at the end of a simple sentence, or directly before whatever specific word it's negating.

Examples

  • Ich verstehe das nicht. (I don't understand that.) — nicht at the end
  • Ich komme nicht aus Berlin. (I'm not from Berlin.) — nicht right before what it negates ("aus Berlin")

Exercises

Try it yourself

1. Rewrite this sentence starting with "Heute" instead of "Ich": Ich trinke Kaffee.
2. Turn this statement into a yes/no question: Du wohnst in München.
3. Spot and correct the mistake: "Morgen ich fahre nach Berlin."
4. Build a question starting with Wo, asking where someone works (arbeiten).
5. Translate into German, keeping the verb in position 2: "Tomorrow I am learning German."
6. Add "nicht" correctly: Ich trinke Bier. → say you don't drink beer.
