Fragepronomen „welch-“ (alle Fälle)

"Which" across all cases: nominative, accusative, dative

Original topic sheet View "Fragepronomen – Welcher Welche Welches (All Cases)" in the Picture Book →

Chapter 35 covered welch- in the nominative. Now that you know the accusative and dative endings from Chapter 14's articles, this table should look very familiar — it's the exact same pattern.

📐 The full welch- table

CaseMasculineFeminineNeuterPlural
Nominativwelcherwelchewelcheswelche
Akkusativwelchenwelchewelcheswelche
Dativwelchemwelcherwelchemwelchen (+n on the noun)

💬 Beispiele im Akkusativ

  • Welchen Mann kennst du? (Which man do you know?)
  • Welches Auto kaufst du? (Which car are you buying?)

💬 Beispiele im Dativ

  • Welchem Freund hilfst du? (Which friend are you helping?)
  • Welcher Frau schenkst du das Buch? (Which woman are you giving the book to?)

📐 Bonus: wessen (genitive — "whose")

Slightly beyond core A1, but useful to recognize: the genitive question word is wessen (whose), and it never changes for gender or number — Wessen Auto ist das? (Whose car is that?), Wessen Kinder sind das? (Whose children are those?) — one word for every situation, which makes it easier than the other three cases.

⚠️ Common mistake: forgetting the verb decides the case

The case of welch- depends entirely on what role the noun plays in the sentence — not on where it sits. Wen kennst du? (kennen takes accusative) needs welchen, but Wem hilfst du? (helfen takes dative, Chapter 19) needs welchem. Check the verb first.

Übungen (Exercises)

✏️ Try it yourself

1. Setzen Sie die richtige Form ein: ___ Film gefällt dir? (der Film — Nominativ)
2. Setzen Sie die richtige Form ein: Ich suche ___ Stift. (der Stift — Akkusativ)
3. Setzen Sie die richtige Form ein: Mit ___ Freundin gehst du? (die Freundin — Dativ, nach "mit")
4. Übersetzen Sie: "Which book are you reading?" (accusative — remember which verb takes the object)
5. Übersetzen Sie mit "wessen": "Whose bag is that?"
6. Write three questions using welch- in three different cases (one nominative, one accusative, one dative), each about a different topic (family, food, city).
