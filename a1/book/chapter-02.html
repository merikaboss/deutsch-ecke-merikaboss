Zahlen

Numbers from zero to millions — plus prices, phone numbers, years and maths

Original topic sheet View "Zahlen" in the Picture Book →

Numbers are everywhere in daily life: prices in a shop, your phone number, your age, a house number, a bus time, the date. They are also one of the most tested things at A1, because the listening exam is full of prices, times and phone numbers. German numbers follow clear rules once you see them, with one famous twist: from 21 upward, German says the numbers "backwards".

1. 0–12: the ones you simply learn

Just like in English, the first twelve numbers have no pattern. Say each one out loud several times. The pronunciation guides use English sounds.

4. 21–99: the "backwards" rule

Unit first, then und, then the ten

German says the last digit first, then und (and), then the ten. It is written as one word. So 21 is literally "one-and-twenty", exactly like the old English nursery rhyme "four-and-twenty blackbirds".

Notice two details in that table. First, 21, 31, 41 and so on use ein, not eins: einunddreißig. Second, inside a bigger number, sechs and sieben keep all their letters (sechsundvierzig, siebenundfünfzig). Only the tens and teens shorten them.

Common mistake: writing down what you hear in the wrong order

When someone says dreiundvierzig, you hear the 3 first, and English speakers often write "34". The number is 43. A trick that works: when you hear a two-digit number, leave a space, write the first digit you hear on the right, then fill in the ten on the left. In the listening exam this one habit saves points.

5. Hundreds, thousands and millions

Big numbers are built by stacking the pieces you already know, from the biggest down, and German writes the whole thing as one word (up to a million).

The trick for reading a long number is to cut it into chunks. Take 3.456: dreitausend + vierhundert + sechsundfünfzig = dreitausendvierhundertsechsundfünfzig. Within each chunk, the "unit before ten" rule still applies.

Two traps with very big numbers

  • Million and Milliarde are nouns: they are written separately, with a capital letter, and take a plural: zwei Millionen Menschen (two million people).
  • False friend: eine Milliarde is an English billion (1,000,000,000). German eine Billion is an English trillion. Getting this wrong changes a number a thousand times over.

7. Numbers in real life

Prices: Wie viel kostet das? (How much does that cost?)

How prices are read

249,00 €zweihundertneunundvierzig Euro

In prices you drop the word Komma and the word Cent: 2,50 € is simply zwei Euro fünfzig. And Euro never takes a plural ending: zehn Euro, never "zehn Euros".

Phone numbers

Phone numbers are read digit by digit or in pairs: 0176 42 89 can be null eins sieben sechs – vier zwei – acht neun or null eins sieben sechs – zweiundvierzig – neunundachtzig. On the phone many people say zwo instead of zwei, so it can't be confused with drei. If you hear zwo, it simply means 2.

Age, house numbers and simple maths

Numbers in sentences

  • Ich bin dreiundzwanzig Jahre alt. (I am 23 years old.)
  • Ich habe zwei Brüder und eine Schwester. (I have two brothers and one sister.)
  • Wir wohnen in der Goethestraße 17. (We live at Goethestraße 17.) — the house number comes after the street name
  • Die Postleitzahl ist 10115. (The postcode is 10115.)
  • Der Bus Nummer 42 fährt zum Bahnhof. (Bus number 42 goes to the station.)

8. eins, ein, eine

Which "one"?

  • eins when counting or when the number stands alone: eins, zwei, drei; Es ist eins. (It's one o'clock.)
  • ein- inside a bigger number: einundzwanzig, einhundert
  • ein / eine directly before a noun, where it also means "a": ein Buch (one book / a book), eine Tasche (one bag / a bag). Its ending changes with gender and case, taught fully in Chapter 11.

A last useful set of words: die Zahl (number, as a quantity), die Nummer (number, as a label: phone, house, room), and die Ziffer (a single digit). So you ask Wie ist Ihre Telefonnummer? (What is your phone number?), never "Telefonzahl".
