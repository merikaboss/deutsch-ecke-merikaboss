Possessivartikel (alle Fälle)

Possessive articles — my, your, his... — across all three cases

Original topic sheet View "Possessivartikel" in the Picture Book →

This chapter does two things at once: it introduces how der/die/das themselves change across the three cases (a piece of grammar the syllabus mentions but doesn't give its own picture — the images jump straight to possessives), and then builds possessive articles on top of that same pattern, since they follow ein/eine/ein's pattern exactly.

First: der/die/das across the three cases

You've met the nominative (subject) case in Chapter 10. Two more cases matter constantly: accusative (the direct object — the thing the action happens to) and dative (the indirect object — the person/thing something is given or done for).

Grammar rule: only the masculine article actually changes in the accusative

CaseMasculineFeminineNeuterPlural
Nominative (subject)derdiedasdie
Accusative (direct object)dendiedasdie
Dative (indirect object)demderdemden (+n on the noun)

Notice: feminine, neuter, and plural stay the same between nominative and accusative — only masculine changes (der → den). All three genders change in the dative.

Examples: spotting the case by the verb's role

  • Der Mann sieht den Hund. (The man sees the dog.) — "der Mann" is the subject (nominative), "den Hund" is what's being seen (accusative)
  • Ich gebe dem Kind einen Ball. (I give the child a ball.) — "dem Kind" is who receives it (dative), "einen Ball" is what's given (accusative)

Now: possessive articles follow the exact same pattern

Possessives (mein, dein, sein...) behave grammatically just like ein — same endings, same case changes, just with a different base word depending on who's the owner.

Grammar rule: possessives take the same endings as "ein"

CaseMasculineFeminineNeuterPlural
Nominativemeinmeinemeinmeine
Accusativemeinenmeinemeinmeine
Dativemeinemmeinermeinemmeinen

Examples

  • Mein Bruder wohnt in Berlin. (My brother lives in Berlin.) — nominative
  • Ich besuche meinen Bruder. (I'm visiting my brother.) — accusative, masculine gets "-en"
  • Ich schreibe meiner Schwester. (I'm writing to my sister.) — dative, feminine gets "-er"
  • Ist das eure Wohnung? (Is that your (plural) apartment?)

Common mistake: forgetting "euer" loses its middle "e"

When euer takes an ending, the second "e" drops out for pronunciation reasons: eure Tasche (your bag), not "euere Tasche." This happens with unser too, though less strictly — unsre is heard, but unsere is also completely correct and more common in writing.

Exercises

Try it yourself

1. Fill in the article: Ich sehe ___ Mann. (I see the man — accusative, masculine)
2. Fill in the article: Ich helfe ___ Frau. (I help the woman — dative, feminine)
3. Say "my sister" in the nominative, then in the accusative.
4. Translate: "I'm visiting his mother."
5. Translate: "Is this your (plural, informal) car?"
6. Fix the mistake: "Das ist euere Tasche."
