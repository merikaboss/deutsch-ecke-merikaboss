W-Fragen und Fragewörter der Zeit

Every question word, wer in all four cases, asking about place and time, and polite indirect questions

Original topic sheet View "W-Fragen und Zeitfragen" in the Picture Book →

W-Fragen starten mit einem Fragewort und fragen nach einer bestimmten Information. (W-questions start with a question word and ask for a specific piece of information.) They're called W-questions because almost all German question words begin with W. As a self-learner you need them twice over: to ask for what you need, and to understand the questions officials, doctors and colleagues ask you. In the A1 speaking exam, part 2 is built entirely around asking and answering them.

1. How a W-question is built

Question word – verb – subject – rest

Position 1Position 2SubjectRest

Wannbeginntder Kurs?

The verb-second rule from Chapter 5 still applies: the question word fills position 1, so the conjugated verb comes immediately after it. There's no helper word like English "do": "Where do you live?" is simply Wo wohnst du? Your voice goes down at the end of a W-question, not up.

Common mistake: keeping statement order

"Wo du wohnst?" and "Was du machst?" are wrong in a direct question. Swap subject and verb: Wo wohnst du? Was machst du? (That order — verb at the end — belongs only in the indirect questions of section 7.)

Three pairs learners mix up

  • wer vs was: was never asks about people. "Who is your brother?" is Wer ist dein Bruder? Was ist dein Bruder? asks what he does ("He's a teacher").
  • wo vs wohin: if there's movement, use wohin. "Wo gehst du?" is wrong; it's Wohin gehst du?
  • wie viel vs wie viele: wie viel with uncountables and money (Wie viel Zeit? Wie viel Geld?), wie viele with plural nouns (Wie viele Tage?).

3. wer in all four cases

Like the articles, wer changes with its job in the sentence. The endings are the same as der: der → wer, den → wen, dem → wem, des → wessen. English has the same system with who / whom / whose, though most people no longer use "whom".

wer – wen – wem – wessen

CaseFormQuestionAnswer
NominativwerWer hilft dir? (Who is helping you?)Mein Bruder.
AkkusativwenWen besuchst du? (Who are you visiting?)Meinen Onkel.
DativwemWem gehört die Tasche? (Who does the bag belong to?)Meiner Kollegin.
GenitivwessenWessen Auto ist das? (Whose car is that?)Das Auto meines Chefs.

The answer is in the same case as the question word: wen → meinen (Akk.), wem → meiner (Dat.). was has only one common form for nominative and accusative: Was ist das? Was kaufst du?

Questions with prepositions: mit wem? or womit?

People vs things

About a person: preposition + wer formAbout a thing: wo(r) + preposition
Mit wem sprichst du? (Who are you talking to?)Womit fährst du? (What are you travelling by?)
Für wen ist das Geschenk? (Who is the present for?)Wofür brauchst du das Geld? (What do you need the money for?)
Von wem ist der Brief? (Who is the letter from?)Wovon lebst du? (What do you live on?)
Über wen sprecht ihr? (Who are you talking about?)Worüber sprecht ihr? (What are you talking about?)

The preposition keeps its case (mit + dative → mit wem; für + accusative → für wen). An r is added before a preposition that starts with a vowel: worüber, woran, worauf.

welch- and was für ein change too

welcher takes the endings of der, and was für ein takes the endings of ein (Chapters 35 and 36 give welch- its full treatment): Welcher Bus fährt zum Zentrum? (Which bus goes to the centre? — Nom.) · Welchen Film siehst du? (Which film are you watching? — Akk.) · Mit welchem Zug kommst du? (Which train are you coming on? — Dat.) · Was für einen Job suchst du? (What kind of job are you looking for?)

4. Place: wo, wohin, woher

Three questions, three kinds of answer

QuestionMeaningTypical answers
Wo?Where is it? (no movement)in Berlin · im Büro · zu Hause · bei meiner Tante · hier · dort
Wohin?Where to? (movement toward)nach Berlin · ins Büro · nach Hause · zu meiner Tante · hierher
Woher?Where from? (origin, movement away)aus Berlin · aus dem Büro · von zu Hause · von meiner Tante

Translations of the answers: in Berlin / in the office / at home / at my aunt's; to Berlin / to the office / home / to my aunt's; from Berlin / out of the office / from home / from my aunt. Notice the home trio: zu Hause (at home) · nach Hause (going home) · von zu Hause (from home).

In speech, wohin and woher are often split: Wo fährst du hin? = Wohin fährst du? · Wo kommst du her? = Woher kommst du? Both are correct.

seit, vor and in — with the right case and tense

WordMeaningExample
seit + Dativsince / for (still continuing)Ich lerne seit zwei Jahren Deutsch. (I've been learning German for two years.)
vor + DativagoIch bin vor zwei Jahren gekommen. (I came two years ago.)
in + Dativin (the future)Der Zug kommt in einer Stunde. (The train arrives in an hour.)
accusative, no prepositionnext / last / everynächsten Montag · letzte Woche · jeden Tag

seit takes the present tense

English says "I have been living here for three years". German uses the present, because the situation is still true now: Ich wohne seit drei Jahren hier. Saying "Ich habe seit drei Jahren hier gewohnt" sounds as if you no longer live there. And "ago" is vor, placed before the time: vor einer Woche, not "eine Woche vor".

7. Polite indirect questions

A direct question to a stranger can sound abrupt. German softens it by putting the question inside a polite opening. The question word then works like weil (Chapter 5): the verb goes to the end.

Direct → indirect

DirectIndirect (more polite)
Wo ist der Bahnhof?Wissen Sie, wo der Bahnhof ist? (Do you know where the station is?)
Wann fährt der Bus?Können Sie mir sagen, wann der Bus fährt? (Can you tell me when the bus leaves?)
Wie viel kostet das?Ich möchte wissen, wie viel das kostet. (I'd like to know how much that costs.)

For a yes/no question, use ob (whether): Wissen Sie, ob die Apotheke heute offen ist? (Do you know whether the pharmacy is open today?)
